# The Boutique Outreach Playbook

*Included free with your Placement Scout database access.*

---

## 1. Why boutiques

Every graduate/placement portal at a bulge-bracket bank or Big Four firm gets thousands of
applications for a handful of roles — some grad schemes report **3,000+ applicants per place**.
You are, statistically, mostly wasting your time there. Not because you're not good enough —
because the odds are built to reject almost everyone regardless of quality.

A boutique M&A shop, a 15-person wealth manager, a 6-person prop desk — these firms don't run
graduate schemes. They don't have a careers portal. When they need someone, they hire when they
notice they need someone, and they hire whoever is in front of them at that moment with a
sensible, specific email. Most of them get **zero to five unsolicited applications a year**, not
three thousand.

The trade-off: boutique roles are less structured, sometimes unpaid or low-paid for the first
placement, and you won't get a slick induction programme. What you get instead: real deal
exposure from week one, direct access to the people making decisions, and a CV line that shows
initiative rather than "I filled in a form like everyone else."

This playbook is about the mechanics of finding these firms and getting a reply — not about
whether boutique experience is "worth it." If you're reading this, you've already decided it is.

## 2. How to read a database row

Every firm in Placement Scout carries the same columns. Here's what each one is actually telling
you:

| Column | What it means | How to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Sector** | M&A / advisory, asset & wealth management, PE/VC, or quant & trading | Match to what you actually want to do — don't spray identical emails across sectors |
| **City** | Registered office location | Boutiques cluster in London, but regional ones (Manchester, Leeds, Edinburgh) have far less competition for the same reply |
| **Website / Careers page** | Direct links — no job board in between | If a "careers page" is just a contact form or doesn't exist, that's a signal: this firm hires informally, which is exactly the kind of firm that responds well to a direct, well-targeted email |
| **FCA authorised** | Whether the firm is regulated by the FCA (Yes/blank) + a link to verify on the public register | Authorisation signals the firm is a real, active regulated business, not a shell or dormant entity — but plenty of legitimate advisory/PE firms are unregulated (M&A advisory often doesn't require FCA authorisation), so don't filter these out |
| **Incorporated** | Year the company was founded | See "picking your 30" below — recency is a strong reply-rate signal |
| **Score** | Our internal boutique-quality score (4–8+) | Rough proxy for "small, active, focused" vs. "large or dormant" — higher isn't automatically better for you, it's a filter to cut noise |
| **Contact** | A verified role inbox (info@, careers@, etc.) where we found one | Useful as a fallback or to confirm the firm is reachable — but never your first choice (see section 4) |

The database is a starting point, not a finished target list. The next section is about cutting
1,100+ firms down to a shortlist you can actually work.

## 3. Picking your 30

Don't email 1,100 firms. Don't even email 100. Response quality collapses the moment your emails
stop being specific to each firm, and generic mail-merged outreach is easy for anyone reading it
to spot and bin.

**The 10/10/10 rule:**
- **10 reach firms** — well-known-in-their-niche, established boutiques you'd be thrilled to hear
  back from. Low reply odds, high upside.
- **10 realistic firms** — small, active, sector-matched, no obvious red flags. This is where most
  of your replies will come from.
- **10 recently-authorised or recently-incorporated firms** — see below, this is the highest-odds
  segment, don't skip it in favour of only chasing recognisable names.

**Filter first by:**
1. **City + sector fit.** If you can't credibly explain why *this* firm in *this* city, don't
   email it yet.
2. **Recency.** A firm authorised or incorporated in the last 12–18 months has no graduate-scheme
   machinery, no backlog of applications, and is actively growing headcount by definition — that's
   exactly the highest-reply-rate segment in the whole database. The weekly "new firms" digest
   exists specifically to surface these before anyone else finds them.
3. **Signs of an actual careers page.** Counterintuitively, a firm with *no* careers page is often
   a better target than one with a generic one — no careers page usually means "we hire when we
   need to, informally," which is precisely the situation your email should walk into.

Thirty well-chosen firms, researched properly, beats a thousand generic ones. This isn't a numbers
game — it's a research game.

## 4. Finding the right person

**Never lead with a generic inbox.** `info@` and `careers@` addresses are triaged by whoever's
free, if they're read at all. At a firm with 5–20 people, there is almost always a named partner,
director, or founder you can find and email directly — and a direct, personal email from a
student who clearly did their homework gets read completely differently to one landing in a
shared inbox.

**Where to find the name:**
1. **The firm's own website** — "Team" or "About" pages at boutiques are usually a full list of
   partners/directors with bios, sometimes even direct emails.
2. **The FCA register's individuals search** — for FCA-authorised firms, the register lists
   approved persons attached to the firm (use the "FCA register" link on each database row, then
   search the firm name on the FCA's public individuals search). This tells you who's formally
   responsible — often the person worth writing to.
3. **LinkedIn triangulation** — search the firm name, filter to people, look for
   Founder/Partner/Director/MD titles. Cross-check against the website team page to confirm you've
   got the right person and current role.
4. **Guessing the email format** — once you have a name, most small firms use a predictable
   pattern (firstname@, firstname.lastname@, f.lastname@). Confirm against any email address you
   *can* see on the site (even a general one) to infer the firm's format, then apply it to the
   name you found.

If, after all that, you genuinely can't find a named person — that's when the role inbox in the
database becomes your fallback, not your default.

## 5. The email

*[Styli: this is the section the outline calls out as needing your real voice and your real
outreach experience — the "moat" over generic career-advice content. Below is a structural
framework so you're not starting from a blank page, but the actual annotated example (or examples)

worked or what you'd change. That's the part nobody else can write for you.]*

**The four-line structure:**

1. **A specific hook about *their* firm.** Not "I'm interested in finance" — something that
   proves you actually looked at this firm specifically. A recent deal, a sector niche, something
   from their website or a news mention.
2. **Why *them*, in one line.** Connect your hook to why you, specifically, want to work at a firm
   like this rather than a bulge-bracket bank — smaller teams, more responsibility, the specific
   sector.
3. **What you bring, in one line.** Not a CV dump — one credible, relevant thing (a course, a
   project, a prior internship, a specific skill) that's actually applicable to what they do.
4. **A small, easy ask.** Not "do you have any openings?" — ask for 15 minutes on a call, or to
   send your CV for whenever something comes up. Low-commitment asks get said yes to far more
   often than "give me a job."

Keep the whole thing under 150 words. Boutique partners read email on their phone between
meetings — length is a filter against you, not a sign of effort.

## 6. The follow-up cadence

Most replies come from the follow-up, not the first email — people are busy, not uninterested.

- **Day 4:** A short, one-line bump. Don't re-explain yourself — just resurface the email
  ("following up in case this got buried — happy to send more info or jump on a quick call").
- **Day 10:** One new angle. A relevant news item, a slightly different ask, or a small piece of
  extra context that wasn't in the first email. This isn't "just checking in again" — it needs to
  give them a new reason to reply.
- **After day 10:** Stop. Move on. A third follow-up reads as pressure, not persistence, and
  burns the relationship for future attempts (e.g. next year).

**Tracking:** use the sheet's own Status / Who / Notes columns as you go —
`Sent → Replied → Call booked → [outcome]`, with the contact name and date in Notes. With 30
firms in motion at once, a spreadsheet is the only way to remember who you followed up with and
when.

## 7. Converting a call into a placement

*[Styli: same note as section 5 — this section is strongest with your own experience of what
actually happened on your calls. Framework below, replace/extend with real examples once you have
them.]*

If you get a call or a meeting, the goal isn't to "interview well" in the traditional sense — most
boutiques aren't running a formal process, so the call itself often decides the outcome.

**Questions worth asking:**
- What does someone in this role actually spend their time on day to day? (Shows you're thinking
  about the substance of the job, not just getting a title.)
- What's a recent piece of work the team's been proud of? (Gives them a chance to talk about
  something they're genuinely engaged in — people respond well to this.)
- Is there a specific gap in the team right now you're trying to fill?

**If there's no formal internship advertised:** don't wait to be offered one — propose it. Once
it's clear there's mutual interest, a direct, low-pressure suggestion ("would a short unpaid
trial period over [dates] be useful for you, so we can both see if this is a good fit?") often
converts genuine interest into an actual placement, because you're removing the friction of them
having to invent a formal process from scratch. Small firms often don't say no to free, capable
help — they just never got around to setting up a scheme for it.
